Q1Circadian preference

Relative frequency distribution · bar chart · pie chart

Reasoning

LarkOwl is categorical. You cannot take a mean of "Lark." The only meaningful summaries are counts and proportions — which is exactly why the question asks for a frequency distribution instead of an average.

The two paragraphs the handout drops after Q1 are not decoration. They are the interpretive setup: past studies find roughly 10% Lark, 20% Owl, 70% Neither, and circadian preference "may not be settled until 22 years of age or later." So when you compare your sample proportions against those benchmarks, the second fact is your explanation for any excess of "Neither" in a college-age sample.

That comparison is one of your strongest candidates for the Q6 summary. Q1 by itself is just counting; the comparison is the statistics.

Q2Depression, anxiety & stress

Histogram of DASScore · shape

Reasoning

DASScore is quantitative, so shape, center and spread are all available now. A histogram answers shape.

Predict before you plot: DASScore is bounded below at 0, and most college students are not severely distressed. A hard floor with room to run upward almost always produces a right skew — a long tail toward high values. Plot it and check whether that holds.

Vocabulary trap

Skew is named for the direction of the tail, not for where the bulk of the data sits. A pile-up on the left with a tail stretching right is skewed right, even though it "leans left" visually.

Scaffold

AKAL
1DASScoreValue
2Minimum=MIN($P$2:$P$254)
3Maximum=MAX($P$2:$P$254)
4Mean=AVERAGE($P$2:$P$254)
5Median=MEDIAN($P$2:$P$254)
Trap · Q2b, Q2c

Take the minimum and maximum from MIN and MAX, not from the histogram's axis. Excel picks round bin edges, so the leftmost bin may start at 0 when your true minimum isn't 0, and the rightmost bin will overshoot your true maximum. The chart is for shape; the formulas are for values.

Skew confirmation · Q2d

Compare AL4 to AL5. Mean > median means a long right tail is pulling the mean up — skewed right. Mean ≈ median means roughly symmetric. Mean < median means skewed left. This is a cross-check on your eyeball reading, not a replacement for it — if the chart and the mean/median disagree, something is wrong.

Chart

Select column P including the header → Insert → Recommended Charts → Histogram. Retitle.

Q3Sleep quality

Full descriptive battery on PoorSleepQuality · fences · boxplot

Reasoning

The whole toolkit on one variable: center (mean, median, mode), spread (SD, range, IQR), position (quartiles), unusual values (fences, outliers) — and then a boxplot that should visually reproduce everything you just computed.

Part (l), "is this consistent with a–k," is the actual point of the question. You are being asked to verify that a picture and a table tell the same story.

Fill down until a value drops below your upper fence. Everything above the fence is an outlier, and those are what you list. Mirror with SMALL(...) if AO14 is nonzero.

Four traps in this block

01 · Sample vs population SD

The question says sample standard deviation. STDEV.S divides by n−1. STDEV.P divides by n and is the wrong answer here.

02 · MODE returns one value

If two values tie for most frequent, Excel silently reports whichever appears first in the column. Worth a sentence in your writeup if you suspect a tie. (If nothing repeated at all, MODE returns #N/A — not a risk with 253 integer scores on a 0–20 scale, but that's what the error would mean.)

03 · QUARTILE may not match your hand calculation

Excel's QUARTILE (identical to QUARTILE.INC) interpolates between data points. Many intro courses teach "the median of the lower half," which behaves more like QUARTILE.EXC and can give a different number.

The assignment tells you to use QUARTILE, so use it — but if your notes define quartiles the other way, say so in one line. A noted discrepancy reads as competence; an unexplained one reads as an error.

04 · The fence rule is inference, not verified

The scaffold above uses Q1 − 1.5×IQR and Q3 + 1.5×IQR, which is the standard formal rule. The question says "the formal rule discussed in class," and there are no MAT 102A notes in this workspace to check against. Confirm against your notes before you commit.

Boxplot (3l)

Select column I including the header → Insert → Charts → Statistical → Box and Whisker.

Required setting

Right-click the box → Format Data Series → check Show outlier points, and Show mean markers while you're there. Excel does not always enable these by default, and without outlier points you cannot answer 3l honestly.

What "consistent" means for your writeup — check all four:

  • Box bottom = your Q1, box top = your Q3, interior line = your median.
  • Whisker ends stop at the most extreme values inside the fences — not at min and max.
  • The plotted outlier dots match exactly the values you listed in 3k.
  • The mean marker sits above the median line if the data is right-skewed. Compare AO2 to AO3.

Q4Stress × circadian preference

Two-way table · conditional distributions · side-by-side bars

Reasoning

Two categorical variables. "Is there a relationship" becomes a sharper question: does the proportion reporting high stress change depending on circadian group? If all three groups report high stress at about the same rate, there is no association. If they differ substantially, there is.

This is where students lose points, and here is exactly why. Parts b, c and d all ask for "a proportion" but use three different denominators:

PartNumeratorDenominatorName
4ball high-stress students253 — everyonemarginal proportion
4cLark and high stressall high-stress studentsconditional on stress
4dLark and high stressall Larksconditional on group

4c and 4d have the same numerator and different denominators, so they produce different numbers. If you can say out loud why "the proportion of high-stress students who are Larks" and "the proportion of Larks who are high-stress" are different questions, you have the skill this question tests.

Only 4d answers "is there a relationship" — it holds the group fixed and compares stress rates across groups.

AR$1 locks the row, so column headers always come from row 1 while the column floats. That's what lets one formula fill in two directions. Get this and two-way tables stop being tedious.

  • Excel guesses which variable becomes the category axis and which becomes the series. If it guesses wrong, use Chart Design → Switch Row/Column. To support your 4d answer you want circadian group on the axis with high/normal as the two series, so the three groups sit side by side for comparison.

Caution worth writing down

Check AR4 — the number of Larks — before you lean on 4e. If that group is small, its high-stress proportion rests on few students and is unstable; a couple of different students would move it noticeably. Naming that limitation is a better answer than stating the winner with false confidence. The handout's own "about 10% are Larks" benchmark tells you what to expect.

Q5Alcohol use & cognition

Side-by-side boxplots · group means · association vs causation

Reasoning

One categorical variable (AlcoholUse, and note it is ordinal — the categories have a natural order) against one quantitative variable (CognitionZscore). Side-by-side boxplots are the right display: one box per group, compared on a shared axis.

CognitionZscore is a z-score, which does you a favor. It's already standardized, so 0 is roughly average performance and the units are standard deviations. A difference of 0.1 between group means is small; 0.8 is large. You need no outside context to judge magnitude.

5f is the heaviest question on the page

The answer is no, and the reason has a name. This is an observational study — students self-reported their drinking; nobody was randomly assigned to drink heavily. So the groups differ in many ways besides alcohol, and any of those could produce a cognition difference. That is confounding.

The abstract printed at the bottom of your handout hands you a concrete confounder: students with later class start times were both more likely to misuse alcohol and different in sleep and academic outcomes. Class schedule is a plausible common cause sitting behind both variables. Naming a specific mechanism beats reciting "correlation is not causation."

Worth one more clause: reverse causation isn't excluded either. Nothing in this design establishes that the drinking came before the cognitive difference.

5g — how to judge "strong"

Don't guess. Use two comparisons:

  1. Spread of the group means against the spread within groups. Your four means sit in BE2:BE5. If the gap between highest and lowest is small compared to the height of a typical box on your chart, the association is weak — alcohol group barely moves cognition relative to how much students within a single group already differ.
  2. Box overlap. If all four boxes overlap heavily on the vertical axis, knowing a student's alcohol group tells you almost nothing about their cognition score. Clear separation would mean a strong association.

Then connect it back: a weak association makes the 5f answer easier. You can't claim causation from an observational study regardless, but it's especially unwarranted when the association itself is small. Note also that Heavy is likely the smallest group — check BC5 — so whatever you observed about it in 5c and 5d rests on few students.

Q6Summary paragraph

One finding per question · results prose, not a walkthrough

Don't re-narrate the case study. This is a results paragraph. What to pull:

FromThe finding worth reporting
Q1Sample circadian breakdown compared against the 10% / 20% benchmark, with the age explanation
Q2Shape of the distress distribution and what it implies about typical vs. extreme students
Q3Center and spread of sleep quality, plus whether outliers exist and what they represent
Q4Whether high-stress rates differ by circadian group — the 4d numbers, with the small-group caution
Q5Direction and size of the cognition difference, plus the explicit no-causation statement

Rules for the prose

  • Every claim carries its number. "Most students were Neither" is weak; the proportion is the finding.
  • Association language only. "Higher alcohol use was associated with lower mean cognition scores" — never "led to," "caused," "reduced," or "affected."
  • Name a limitation. Observational design, self-reported drinking and stress, single institution, small subgroups. One sentence is enough and it reads as competence.
  • Past tense, third person, and no "I clicked Insert → Chart."
