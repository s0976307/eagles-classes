BOOK SUMMARY: GEORGE W. BUSH (James Mann, The American Presidents Series)

MAIN THESIS
- Appearances were deceiving. Bush posed as a plain-spoken Texan, but he came from wealth, prep schools, Yale, and political dynasty. He was unpopular with millions yet charming in person; caricatured as dim, yet canny.
- Two self-chosen, far-reaching decisions defined the presidency: the tax cuts (2001, 2003) and the Iraq War (2003). Both had enormous, lasting costs. Mann calls Iraq "a strategic blunder of epic proportions."
- First-term style: simple positions, refusal to negotiate, heavy reliance on advisers (his father's team, Cheney), and a "war on terror" doctrine built on preemption. In the second term he became more hands-on and pragmatic, leaning on Rice and Gates.
- He was not risk-averse (his own words, quoted from Gates). Some gambles paid off; the country paid heavily for the rest.
- Mann separates judgments that need time (counterterrorism, NSA surveillance) from those that can be judged now (Iraq, tax cuts, deficits). Future historians will likely rank him low, though PEPFAR is his most admired achievement.

CHAPTER-BY-CHAPTER SUMMARY

Prologue
Bush was only the second president whose father had also been president (after John Quincy Adams). He lived in his father's shadow ("Little George," "Junior," "W"). The 9/11 attacks ended the post-Cold War calm; his presidency moved from peak American power (2000) to economic and strategic trouble (2008).

Ch. 1: "A Good-Time Guy" (1946-1975)
Born July 6, 1946, in New Haven. Raised in Midland, Texas, he built a West Texas identity. His sister Robin died of leukemia in 1953, and he grew close to his mother, Barbara. He attended Andover, cultivating a mischievous, party-loving image, then Yale (1964-68), where he joined Delta Kappa Epsilon and resented Yale "snobs." He joined the Texas Air National Guard, a "champagne unit," with help from family friends; the Guard service became a lasting controversy. In the early 1970s he drifted: an Alabama campaign, Harvard Business School (1973-75), then oil work in Midland. He founded Arbusto (1977; it became the "Shrub" joke). His 1978 House loss to Kent Hance taught him to "play rougher" and never let opponents define him. He met Karl Rove that year.

Ch. 2: "The Rising Politician" (1976-1994)
A 1976 DUI arrest in Maine preceded his marriage to Laura Welch (1977). In 1986, at forty, he quit drinking and embraced evangelical Christianity, partly because his father was preparing a 1988 presidential run. In 1987 he moved to Washington and served as the family's "loyalty enforcer" under Lee Atwater, whose negative campaign helped defeat Dukakis. He bought into the Texas Rangers (1989). After his father lost to Clinton in 1992, Bush ran for governor of Texas and defeated Ann Richards in 1994, using Rove's disciplined message strategy and the label "compassionate conservative."

Ch. 3: "The New President and His Tax Cuts" (2000-2003)
Bush won through Bush v. Gore (5-4, December 12, 2000). He promised civility but governed with a hard line. The first tax cut (2001, about $1.35 trillion over ten years) lowered the top rate to 35 percent and phased out the estate tax. Greenspan's support was conditional, which received little attention. Treasury Secretary Paul O'Neill wanted triggers or a one-time rebate and was later forced out. Bush's refrain: "We'll be negotiating with ourselves." Senator Jeffords's defection (May 2001) handed the Senate to the Democrats and showed the GOP moving right. The 2003 tax cut, passed during war, cut dividend and capital-gains taxes; Cheney broke the Senate tie. The FY2000 surplus of $86 billion became a $642 billion deficit by FY2008. Other early moves: rejecting Kyoto, reviving the Mexico City policy, limiting stem-cell funding (August 2001), and No Child Left Behind (with Ted Kennedy, signed 2002).

Ch. 4: "September 11" (2000-2002)
Bush's foreign-policy team were his father's veterans ("retreads"): Cheney, Powell, Rice, Rumsfeld, Wolfowitz, Armitage. Their rivalries were largely hidden. The administration focused on nation-states and missile defense and paid little attention to al-Qaeda, despite warnings from Clinton, Clarke, and Tenet. The August 6, 2001 PDB ("Bin Laden Determined to Strike in U.S.") came too late. After 9/11, Cheney ran the response from the bunker. Bush chose Afghanistan over Wolfowitz's push for Iraq, with "boots on the ground" and a "coalition of the willing" instead of NATO. Secret measures followed: the Patriot Act, warrantless NSA surveillance (authorized October 4, 2001), military tribunals, Guantanamo (chosen to avoid U.S. courts), CIA black sites, and "enhanced interrogation," including waterboarding (Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, 183 times). Bush often ratified plans drawn up by Cheney and his legal team.

Ch. 5: "Iraq" (2002-2003)
Bush framed a "second phase" of the war on terror. The "axis of evil" speech (January 2002) named Iran, Iraq, and North Korea and alienated Europe. Bush and Rice modeled their doctrine on Truman. The West Point speech (June 2002) and the National Security Strategy (September 2002) endorsed preemption. Scowcroft's op-ed "Don't Attack Saddam" was the main establishment dissent; Cheney pushed for action, and Bush chose the UN route. Congress authorized force (Senate 77-23, House 296-133), and UN Resolution 1441 passed 15-0 (November 8, 2002). Powell's February 5, 2003 UN speech relied on flawed intelligence. The second UN resolution failed; Bush gave up at the Azores (March 16). The invasion began March 20, and Baghdad fell within three weeks. Mann lists WMD as the public reason, but also the post-9/11 "mind-set" (Saddam as a future threat), a desire to show U.S. power, and hopes of pressuring Libya and others. Tenet's "slam dunk" remark is discussed.

Ch. 6: "Reelection and Its Unhappy Aftermath" (2003-2006)
Rove framed 2004 around the "strong wartime leader." Kerry became the Democratic nominee. Domestic policy included Sarbanes-Oxley (2002), Medicare Part D (2003, passed 220-215 after Bush's late-night lobbying), and PEPFAR ($15 billion over five years). Iraq deteriorated: Bremer disbanded the army and purged Baathists; Saddam was captured (December 2003), but no WMD were found. Abu Ghraib photos (2004) were a "low point." DOJ lawyers threatened mass resignations over the NSA program, and Bush backed down: "I never wanted to be blindsided like that again." Kerry's "I actually did vote for the $87 billion" let Bush paint him as a flip-flopper, and Bush won reelection. Social Security privatization failed in 2005. Katrina (August 2005) exposed a slow federal response, an unqualified FEMA chief, and the "Brownie" remark. Miers's nomination failed; Roberts and Alito were confirmed. Libby was indicted (2005). Hamdan v. Rumsfeld (June 29, 2006) struck down the military commissions. Sectarian war erupted in Iraq; Rice: "It's failing." Democrats won both houses in the 2006 midterms.

Ch. 7: "Second-Term Changes" (2006-2008)
Gates replaced Rumsfeld. Cheney lost influence; Rice became the main foreign-policy operator. Bush sided with Rice on diplomacy: he rejected a U.S. strike on Syria's nuclear reactor (Israel bombed it itself, September 2007), backed a Lebanon ceasefire, and opened talks with Iran, Syria, and North Korea. The North Korea deal failed, and Treasury's financial sanctions became the lasting tool. The "freedom agenda" (second inaugural: "ending tyranny in our world") faltered in Egypt and after Hamas's 2006 win. The India nuclear deal made Bush very popular there. He ordered the surge (January 10, 2007) and counterinsurgency under Petraeus over the objections of the Joint Chiefs and Rice, calling it "the toughest and most unpopular decision of my presidency." Violence and U.S. deaths fell by mid-2008. A 2008 agreement set a deadline for U.S. withdrawal by the end of 2011.

Ch. 8: "I'm Going to Be Roosevelt, Not Hoover" (2008-2009)
Bush let Treasury Secretary Henry Paulson (ex-Goldman Sachs CEO) run the crisis response. Mann traces its roots to deregulation that began before Bush, which Bush accelerated. Steps: the Bear Stearns rescue (March 2008), the Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac takeover (September 2008), and the decision to let Lehman Brothers fail (September 15). The AIG rescue followed within days. Bush's principles were inconsistent: "Saving AIG would look like a glaring contradiction. But that was a hell of a lot better than a financial collapse." The $700 billion TARP was rejected by the House (228-205) on September 29, after the Dow fell 778 points, then passed 263-171 on October 3. Bush also convened the first G-20 leaders' summit (November 2008), approved auto bailouts (December 2008), and refused Cheney's push for a Libby pardon. He left office January 20, 2009, with two wars and a deep recession.

Epilogue: Life After the White House
Bush was private: a memoir, Decision Points (2010), and a Dallas library (2013) that asks visitors "You decide." Obama called him "a good man." PEPFAR is the most admired legacy. Bush gave paid speeches (at least $15 million by 2011), avoided attacking Obama, backed immigration reform, and took up painting. Mann's verdict: Iraq and the tax cuts are clear failures; counterterror measures need more time.

KEY PEOPLE
- George H. W. Bush: father; 41st president; oil man, CIA director, UN ambassador.
- Barbara Bush: mother; grew close to George W. after Robin's death.
- Laura Bush: wife (m. 1977); librarian; campaigned for democracy in Burma.
- Jeb Bush: brother; governor of Florida (elected 1998).
- Lee Atwater: 1988 campaign strategist and mentor; pioneer of negative campaigning.
- Karl Rove: political adviser from 1978; architect of the 2000 and 2004 campaigns.
- Ann Richards: Texas governor Bush defeated in 1994.
- Bob Bullock: Texas Democratic lieutenant governor; bipartisan ally.
- Ted Kennedy: Senate Democrat who co-wrote No Child Left Behind.
- Paul O'Neill: first Treasury secretary; opposed permanent tax cuts; forced out.
- Dick Cheney: vice president; driving force behind secret programs and hawkish policy.
- Alan Greenspan: Fed chairman; conditional support for the 2001 tax cut.
- Condoleezza Rice: national security adviser, then secretary of state; the main diplomat of the second term.
- Colin Powell: secretary of state; cautioned on Iraq; made the February 2003 UN case.
- Donald Rumsfeld: defense secretary (2001-06); removed after the 2006 midterms.
- Paul Wolfowitz: deputy defense secretary; neoconservative who argued for Iraq first.
- George Tenet: CIA director; "slam dunk" on WMD.
- Brent Scowcroft: former national security adviser to Bush's father; wrote "Don't Attack Saddam."
- John McCain: 2000 primary rival; 2008 Republican nominee.
- L. Paul Bremer: head of the Coalition Provisional Authority (2003-04).
- David Petraeus: commander of the 2007 surge.
- Robert Gates: defense secretary (2006-09); a realist.
- Henry Paulson: treasury secretary (2006-09) who led the financial crisis response.
- Michael Brown: FEMA director during Katrina, a political appointee without relevant experience.
- I. Lewis "Scooter" Libby: Cheney's chief of staff; convicted of perjury; sentence commuted; no pardon.
- James Comey / Robert Mueller: Justice Department and FBI leaders who threatened to resign over the NSA program.

LIKELY TEST QUESTIONS (short answers)
1. Why are appearances "frequently deceiving" in Bush's case? He styled himself a common Texan but came from elite privilege; he was unpopular yet personally charming; caricatured as dim but shrewd.
2. How did his father shape him? He spent his life defining himself against, and seeking approval from, his father. Robin's death drew him close to his mother, and his temperament was less driven and more mischievous.
3. Why did Bush quit drinking and turn to evangelical religion? Personal reasons, plus concern that his drinking could embarrass his father during the 1988 campaign; he also courted evangelical voters.
4. What did the 1978 race teach him? To play rougher, define opponents first, and never let them define him.
5. Why does the 2001 tax cut matter? It was his most important domestic law. It cut top rates and the estate tax, relied on an overly optimistic surplus, and helped turn surpluses into deficits and widen income gaps.
6. What was Jeffords's defection? A Vermont Republican left the GOP after Bush refused more special-education money, giving the Democrats the Senate; Mann uses it to show the party's rightward shift.
7. How was No Child Left Behind passed? Bush dropped private-school vouchers, and Kennedy accepted national testing and standards. Critics later blamed it for excessive test prep.
8. What were the main secret counterterrorism programs? NSA warrantless surveillance, military commissions, Guantanamo detention outside U.S. courts, CIA black sites, and enhanced interrogation including waterboarding.
9. What was the preemption doctrine? Announced at West Point (2002) and in the 2002 National Security Strategy: the U.S. would act preemptively, even alone, replacing containment and deterrence.
10. What were Bush's reasons for Iraq? WMD was the public reason. Mann adds the post-9/11 "mind-set" (Saddam as a future threat), hawks' desire to show U.S. power, and hopes of pressuring Libya and others.
11. Why did the Iraq War go wrong? Misjudgments about allied support, costs ($100 billion predicted; over $2 trillion spent), Iraqi reactions ("greeted as liberators"), troop levels, de-Baathification and disbanding the army, and the absence of WMD.
12. What does "Mission Accomplished" symbolize? Premature triumphalism. Bush later called the banner "a big mistake."
13. What was the NSA showdown? DOJ lawyers judged parts of the program illegal; threatened mass resignations forced Bush to back down, and he resented being "blindsided."
14. What went wrong with Katrina? A slow federal response, bureaucratic disputes with Louisiana, an unqualified FEMA chief, and delayed decisions. Bush admitted, "I took too long to decide."
15. Why did Bush bail out financial firms? To avoid a second Great Depression ("Roosevelt, not Hoover"). He abandoned his anti-bailout rhetoric; Mann reads this as necessity, not principle.
16. What caused the 2008 crisis? Deregulation dating to the 1970s and 1990s, which Bush accelerated, plus subprime mortgages, securitization, derivatives, and weak oversight.
17. What changed in the second term? Rice drove diplomacy; Bush ordered the surge over objections; he engaged Iran, Syria, and North Korea and signed the India nuclear deal. He became more hands-on and pragmatic.
18. What was the freedom agenda, and where did it fail? A push to spread democracy ("ending tyranny in our world," 2005). It stalled in Egypt, after Hamas won in Palestine, and in China, where Bush was selective.
19. What is Mann's overall verdict? Iraq and the tax cuts were Bush's own choices and are likely to be judged failures. Counterterror measures need more time. PEPFAR is his most admired legacy.
