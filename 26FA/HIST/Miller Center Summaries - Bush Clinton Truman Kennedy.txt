GEORGE W. BUSH (43rd President)
Essays by Gary L. Gregg II (overview author). Chicago citation: Miller Center of Public Affairs, University of Virginia. "George W. Bush." https://millercenter.org/president/gwbush
==================================================================

LIFE IN BRIEF (https://millercenter.org/president/gwbush/life-in-brief)
- Took office in 2001 as only the second son of a president (after George H.W. Bush). Won the Electoral College despite losing the popular vote to Al Gore.
- Born July 6, 1946. Served in the Texas Air National Guard during Vietnam, without seeing combat. Worked in the oil industry; married Laura Welch in 1977.
- Lost a 1978 U.S. House race. Defeated Ann Richards for Texas governor in 1994; reelected in 1998 by a landslide.
- "Compassionate conservatism": faith-based initiatives and education reform, including No Child Left Behind (2001). Signed the Medicare Modernization Act (2003), adding prescription drug benefits.
- Congress passed $1.35 trillion in tax cuts, but they were temporary. Deficits grew; the economy collapsed in 2008 (the Great Recession).
- September 11, 2001 attacks (nearly 3,000 killed) shifted focus to foreign threats. "War on terror."
- Afghanistan invaded October 2001 (the longest war in U.S. history). Iraq invaded March 2003; no weapons of mass destruction found; war grew unpopular.
- Approval fell sharply. Republicans lost Congress in 2006 and the White House in 2008.

LIFE BEFORE THE PRESIDENCY (https://millercenter.org/president/gwbush/life-before-the-presidency)
- Born in New Haven, CT, to George H.W. Bush and Barbara Pierce. Family moved to Midland, TX, in 1950.
- Sister Robin died of leukemia in 1953.
- Attended Phillips Academy (Andover), then Yale (history; Skull and Bones), graduating 1968.
- Texas Air National Guard service (later criticized). MBA from Harvard. Founded Arbusto Energy in 1977.
- Married Laura Welch November 5, 1977. Twin daughters Barbara and Jenna born 1981.
- Quit drinking at age 40. Faith deepened after a conversation with Billy Graham.
- Lost 1978 House race. Helped father's 1988 campaign. Bought the Texas Rangers in 1989 (sold 1998).
- Defeated Ann Richards in 1994; reelected 1998 with a record 69% of the vote.

CAMPAIGNS AND ELECTIONS (https://millercenter.org/president/gwbush/campaigns-and-elections)
- Decided to run after 1998 reelection; asked Karl Rove to prepare a national campaign.
- Primaries: won Iowa, lost New Hampshire to John McCain, rebounded in South Carolina.
- Running mate Dick Cheney.
- 2000: Strong debates against Gore, but a DUI disclosure five days before the election cost him his lead.
- Florida recount dispute; Supreme Court ruled 5-4 in Bush v. Gore (December 2000). Electoral College 271-266; Gore won the popular vote.
- 2004: defeated John Kerry. Won 286 electoral votes to 251 and 50.73% of the popular vote.
- Second-term setbacks: Iraq casualties, Hurricane Katrina response (2005), scandals. The 2006 midterms cost his party both houses.

DOMESTIC AFFAIRS (https://millercenter.org/president/gwbush/domestic-affairs)
- Jan. 29, 2001: created the Office of Faith-Based and Community Initiatives by executive order.
- Aug. 2001: limited federal stem cell funding to existing embryonic stem cell lines.
- Tax cuts: about $1.6 trillion proposed, narrowed to $1.35 trillion; second round in 2003.
- No Child Left Behind signed January 8, 2002 (100% proficiency goal by 2013-2014).
- Medicare Modernization Act signed December 8, 2003; largest Medicare expansion since 1965.
- Social Security partial privatization push after 2004; dropped in 2005.
- Supreme Court: John Roberts became chief justice (2005); Samuel Alito confirmed after Harriet Miers withdrew.
- Hurricane Katrina (August 29, 2005): heavy criticism of federal response; "Brownie, you're doing a heck of a job."
- 2008 financial crisis: signed the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act (October 2008), creating the $700 billion TARP.

FOREIGN AFFAIRS (https://millercenter.org/president/gwbush/foreign-affairs)
- 9/11 expanded presidential power; approval reportedly reached about 90%.
- Learned of the attacks at an elementary school in Sarasota, FL.
- Congress authorized force September 18, 2001; air strikes in Afghanistan began October 7. Kandahar fell December 7, 2001.
- Bush Doctrine (September 2002 National Security Strategy): preventive war, unilateral action, spreading democracy.
- Iraq: bombing campaign began March 19, 2003.
- Surge: General David Petraeus oversaw about 20,000 additional troops from January 2007.
- Detainees held at Guantanamo Bay; enhanced interrogation methods; NSA warrantless surveillance. Hamdan v. Rumsfeld (2006) ruled against military tribunals.
- Foreign aid: PEPFAR (HIV/AIDS, $15 billion proposed 2003); Millennium Challenge Account; President's Malaria Initiative (2005).

LIFE AFTER THE PRESIDENCY (https://millercenter.org/president/gwbush/life-after-the-presidency)
- Left office at age 62 with a 33% approval rating. Returned to Texas (Crawford ranch, then Dallas, near SMU).
- Largely avoided politics; did not publicly criticize his successor.
- George W. Bush Presidential Center at SMU; Bush Institute.
- Memoir Decision Points (2010). Published 41 (2014), a biography of his father.
- Took up painting. Fundraising for wounded veterans; PTSD advocacy.

FAMILY LIFE (https://millercenter.org/president/gwbush/family-life)
- Oldest of five siblings. Grandfather Prescott Bush was a U.S. senator from Connecticut.
- Father: vice president (1981-1989) and president (1989-1993). Brother Jeb: Florida governor (1999-2007).
- Married Laura Welch in 1977. Her "perfect balance" to his more outgoing style.
- Twins Jenna and Barbara born 1981; both campaigned for his 2004 reelection.
- Bought Crawford, TX, ranch in 1999 ("Western White House").
- Grandchildren: Mila (2013) and Poppy (2015).

THE AMERICAN FRANCHISE (https://millercenter.org/president/gwbush/the-american-franchise)
- Promised a "humble" foreign policy; "Mission Accomplished" speech aboard USS Abraham Lincoln (May 1, 2003).
- Gore conceded after the Florida recount; Bush thanked Gore in his inaugural address for a contest "ended with grace."
- Democrats questioned his legitimacy (Terence McAuliffe, Gloria Steinem, Michael Moore).
- Limited bipartisanship: appointed only one Democrat (Norman Mineta) to his cabinet.
- Jim Jeffords left the GOP in May 2001, giving Democrats a 51-49 Senate majority.
- Growing polarization: gerrymandering and fragmented institutions.

IMPACT AND LEGACY (https://millercenter.org/president/gwbush/impact-and-legacy)
- Lost the popular vote in 2000; Bush v. Gore remains controversial.
- No Child Left Behind: most impactful education change in a generation; replaced in 2015.
- 9/11 response: about 90% approval.
- Afghanistan: longest U.S. war. Osama bin Laden killed in 2011 (under Obama).
- Iraq: no WMDs found; insurgency; over 4,000 American deaths in the following eight years. Patriot Act divisive.
- Tax cuts not made permanent; deficits fueled the Tea Party.
- Failed reforms: Social Security privatization, immigration reform.
- Final months: stock market crash, bailouts, recession.
- Essay calls him a "lightning rod for controversy."

BILL CLINTON (42nd President)
Essays by Russell L. Riley (Miller Center). Chicago citation: Miller Center of Public Affairs, University of Virginia. "Bill Clinton." https://millercenter.org/president/clinton
==================================================================

LIFE IN BRIEF (https://millercenter.org/president/clinton/life-in-brief)
- Born 1946 after his father died; grew up in Arkansas. Worked on Senator Fulbright's committee staff. Rhodes scholar at Oxford; Yale Law School.
- Lost a 1974 U.S. House race; elected Arkansas attorney general; at age 32 (1978) became the nation's youngest governor. Later four more terms.
- 1992: defeated George H.W. Bush and Ross Perot. "New Democrat" approach aimed at the middle class.
- Health care plan (led by Hillary Clinton) rejected by Congress. 1994: Republicans won both houses for the first time since 1955.
- 1996: reelected after a government shutdown; first Democrat since FDR to win reelection.
- Impeachment: House impeached him for lying under oath and obstruction over his affair with intern Monica Lewinsky. Senate acquitted him (February 12, 1999). Whitewater did not link him or the First Lady to crimes.
- Foreign affairs: Northern Ireland peace talks; ended Haiti's military rule; NATO bombing halted Serbian attacks in Bosnia and Kosovo. Did not stop the Rwandan genocide.

LIFE BEFORE THE PRESIDENCY (https://millercenter.org/president/clinton/life-before-the-presidency)
- Born in Hope, Arkansas, August 19, 1946. Raised partly by grandmother Edith Cassidy.
- Stepfather Roger Clinton (abusive, alcoholic car dealer). Family moved to Hot Springs in 1950.
- Met President John F. Kennedy in July 1963 (Boys Nation).
- Georgetown (1964), then clerked for Senator J. William Fulbright (Vietnam critic). Rhodes Scholarship to Oxford; no degree there.
- Draft: avoided service; lottery number 311 kept him from being called.
- Yale Law (1973); married Hillary Rodham in 1975; lost 1974 House race.
- Attorney general (1976); governor (1978, age 32); lost in 1980; returned in 1982.
- National Governors Association chair (1986-1987); Democratic Leadership Council centrist push. Poorly received 1988 convention speech.

CAMPAIGNS AND ELECTIONS (https://millercenter.org/president/clinton/campaigns-and-elections)
- 1992 primaries: survived draft and affair questions.
- 1992 general: Bush lost support after breaking the "no new taxes" pledge. James Carville: "It's the economy, stupid."
- Perot won about 19% of the popular vote, no electoral votes.
- 1994 midterms: Republicans took Congress after the health care failure; Newt Gingrich's "Contract with America."
- Scandals: Whitewater, death of Vincent Foster, Paula Jones.
- 1995-1996 comeback: GOP overreach on Medicare; Dick Morris advised a middle-class pivot (crime, welfare reform).
- 1996: defeated Bob Dole, winning 33 states; minority of the popular vote.

DOMESTIC AFFAIRS (https://millercenter.org/president/clinton/domestic-affairs)
- Succeeded on every goal except health care reform.
- 1993 economic package passed with no Republican votes. Deficit fell from $290 billion (1992) to $203 billion (1994); $124 billion surplus by 1999.
- NAFTA passed 1993. 1996 welfare overhaul ("end welfare as we know it") replaced AFDC with block grants. Minimum wage rose to $5.15.
- "Don't ask, don't tell" compromise.
- Ruth Bader Ginsburg appointed to the Supreme Court in 1993.
- Health care: Hillary Clinton's task force; "Harry and Louise" ads; dropped August 1994.
- Clinton v. Jones (May 27, 1997): 9-0 ruling that Paula Jones's suit could proceed.
- Admitted "inappropriate" conduct August 17, 1998. House adopted two impeachment articles; Senate acquitted February 12, 1999. Republicans lost five House seats in 1998.

FOREIGN AFFAIRS (https://millercenter.org/president/clinton/foreign-affairs)
- Somalia: after U.S. soldiers' bodies were dragged through Mogadishu (October 1993), he withdrew by March 1994.
- Rwanda: about 800,000 killed in 1994; U.S. and UN did not act decisively. Clinton apologized in 1998.
- Haiti: Jimmy Carter, Colin Powell, and Sam Nunn negotiated Cedras's departure under threat of invasion.
- "Doctrine of enlargement": expand market democracies through free trade, multilateral peacekeeping, and intervention when practical.
- Successes: Russia withdrew from Estonia and Latvia (1994); NAFTA (1993); GATT revision (1994); helped avert a 1995 Mexican economic collapse; George Mitchell's Northern Ireland talks.
- Bosnia: NATO bombing; Richard Holbrooke's Dayton Accords; about 20,000 U.S. troops in NATO peacekeeping.
- Kosovo (1999): NATO bombing forced Serbian withdrawal; no U.S. battle casualties.
- Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic joined NATO. Nunn-Lugar program secured Russia's nuclear arsenal.

LIFE AFTER THE PRESIDENCY (https://millercenter.org/president/clinton/life-after-the-presidency)
- Settled in Chappaqua, NY. Kept a New York City office; busy speaking schedule.
- Wrote a memoir; oversaw the presidential library in Little Rock, AR.
- Clinton Presidential Foundation: HIV/AIDS, racial and ethnic reconciliation, economic empowerment.
- Heavily involved in Hillary Clinton's 2016 campaign.

FAMILY LIFE (https://millercenter.org/president/clinton/family-life)
- Daughter Chelsea, born February 27, 1980 (name from Joni Mitchell's "Chelsea Morning").
- Clintons tried to raise Chelsea away from media attention. She attended private school in Washington.
- Chelsea graduated from Stanford; often traveled with her parents.
- Hillary's father died April 1993; Bill's mother died less than a year later.
- Allegations of misconduct followed him (Gennifer Flowers, Paula Jones).
- After testifying to a grand jury, he gave a televised apology: "Nothing is more important to me personally." Critics found it insufficient.

THE AMERICAN FRANCHISE (https://millercenter.org/president/clinton/the-american-franchise)
- Population over 280 million. About 69.1% white, 12.1% Black, 12.5% Hispanic (Hispanics fastest-growing group).
- National Voter Registration Act (1993, "Motor Voter"): voter registration at motor vehicle offices.
- Turbulent 1990s: O.J. Simpson trial deepened racial divides.
- Proposition 187 (California); Proposition 209 ended state minority assistance programs; Hopwood v. Texas limited affirmative action.
- Domestic terrorism: 1993 WTC bombing; 1995 Oklahoma City bombing (168 killed); 1996 Atlanta Olympics bombing.
- Million Man March (October 1995). Clinton created a presidential Commission on Race.
- Gay rights and conservative backlash.

IMPACT AND LEGACY (https://millercenter.org/president/clinton/impact-and-legacy)
- Riley: legacy is still hard to judge, but the "New Democrat" approach reshaped the Democratic Party and undercut the "Reagan Revolution."
- Clinton adopted some Reagan-era appeals (law and order, welfare reform) while keeping Democratic commitments to the disadvantaged.
- Republicans gained Congress during his presidency; Gore lost the 2000 election.
- Fiscal discipline and budget surpluses; historians may weigh the failed health care overhaul more heavily.
- Free-trade accomplishments: NAFTA, GATT. Helped manage currency crises in Mexico and East Asia.
- Balkans intervention seen as a likely success; Rwanda a major criticism.
- Second president impeached; damage to his historical standing greater than harm to the institution.

HARRY S. TRUMAN (33rd President)
Essays by Alonzo L. Hamby (Professor Emeritus, Ohio University). Chicago citation: Miller Center of Public Affairs, University of Virginia. "Harry S. Truman." https://millercenter.org/president/truman
==================================================================

LIFE IN BRIEF (https://millercenter.org/president/truman/life-in-brief)
- Took office April 12, 1945, after FDR died; served nearly eight years. Historians now rank him among the nation's best presidents.
- Born May 8, 1884, in Lamar, Missouri; raised in Independence. Poor eyesight blocked West Point; finances prevented college.
- Farmed 1906-1914. Married Bess Wallace in 1919; daughter Mary Margaret born 1924.
- WWI: National Guard unit in France; turned an artillery battery into a capable unit.
- Kansas City Democratic boss Tom Pendergast backed his political rise. Jackson County presiding judge (1926-1934); U.S. Senate (1934). Chaired the "Truman Committee" on defense spending.
- 1944: FDR's running mate; 82 days as vice president. As president, approved atomic bombings of Hiroshima (August 6) and Nagasaki (August 9, 1945).
- Cold War containment. Taft-Hartley labor law passed over his veto.
- 1948: defeated Thomas Dewey in an upset. "Fair Deal" mostly stalled.
- Korean War (1950); did not seek reelection in 1952; Eisenhower won.

LIFE BEFORE THE PRESIDENCY (https://millercenter.org/president/truman/life-before-the-presidency)
- Born May 8, 1884, in Lamar, MO; moved to Independence in 1890.
- Reading and piano in youth; high school graduate (1901); no college.
- Worked for the Kansas City Star, a construction company, and a bank; farmed in Grandview from 1906. Joined National Guard in 1905.
- WWI: captain in the 129th Field Artillery; fought in the Vosges and Argonne. Married Bess Wallace June 28, 1919.
- Haberdashery failed (1922). Pendergast backed him for Jackson County judge.
- 1934: U.S. Senate, arriving December 31, 1934. Led a 1941 Senate committee on wasteful defense spending.
- 1944: Roosevelt chose him as running mate. On April 12, 1945, Eleanor Roosevelt told him, "Harry, the president is dead."

CAMPAIGNS AND ELECTIONS (https://millercenter.org/president/truman/campaigns-and-elections)
- 1946 midterms: Democrats lost both chambers. Approval 35% (December 1946).
- Vetoed Taft-Hartley; backed civil rights; recognized Israel (May 1948).
- Henry Wallace's Progressive Party (January 1948). Dixiecrat revolt after a strong civil rights plank; Strom Thurmond's States' Rights ticket.
- Ran against the "do-nothing" Republican 80th Congress. 15-day whistle-stop train tour.
- Dewey (with Earl Warren) was the favorite in polls.
- Result: 49.5% of the popular vote to Dewey's 45.1%; 303 electoral votes to 189. Rebuilt the New Deal coalition. "Dewey Defeats Truman" photo.

DOMESTIC AFFAIRS (https://millercenter.org/president/truman/domestic-affairs)
- Inherited the end of WWII, postwar conversion, and a divided party.
- Clark Clifford and John Steelman as key aides; Truman largely served as his own chief of staff.
- September 1945 message: public works, higher minimum wage, national health insurance. Only the Employment Act of 1946 passed.
- 1945-1946 strikes; approval fell to 32% by September 1946.
- Taft-Hartley vetoed June 1947; Congress overrode him.
- Fair Deal (1949): public housing, higher minimum wage, expanded Social Security passed.
- Korean War economics: Defense Production Act (December 1950). Seized the steel industry (April 1952); Supreme Court ruled 6-3 against him.
- Loyalty program (1946); Joseph McCarthy's attacks; scandals (Harry Vaughan; J. Howard McGrath).
- Announced March 29, 1952, he would not seek another term.

FOREIGN AFFAIRS (https://millercenter.org/president/truman/foreign-affairs)
- Cabinet: James F. Byrnes (State), then George C. Marshall (1947), then Dean Acheson (1949).
- National Security Act (1947): created the CIA and the National Security Council.
- Endorsed atomic bombings of Japan; surrender followed in August 1945.
- Tensions with Stalin over Poland and Potsdam (July 1945).
- Truman Doctrine (March 1947) for Greece and Turkey; Marshall Plan (1947); Berlin airlift (1948-1949); NATO formed 1949.
- Soviet atomic test (1949); hydrogen bomb approved January 1950; NSC-68.
- Mao proclaimed the People's Republic of China (October 1, 1949). North Korea invaded South Korea June 25, 1950. Fired General MacArthur in 1951.
- Israel: backed UN partition (November 1947); U.S. first to recognize Israel (May 15, 1948).

LIFE AFTER THE PRESIDENCY (https://millercenter.org/president/truman/life-after-the-presidency)
- Lived 19 years after leaving office. Returned to Independence, MO, with Bess.
- Chose Independence for his presidential library.
- Memoirs (1955, 1956), partly written by ghostwriters; sold well.
- Publicly opposed Eisenhower; campaigned against him in 1956. Got along better with Kennedy and Johnson; never with Nixon.
- Died December 26, 1972. Buried at the library. Bess's marker read "First Lady of the United States."

FAMILY LIFE (https://millercenter.org/president/truman/family-life)
- Mother Martha Ellen ("Mama") was a loyal Democrat; she died in 1947.
- Sister Mary Jane never married; brother Vivian (FHA district director) advised him on Missouri politics.
- Daughter Margaret: toured as a singer in 1947; Truman's December 1950 threatening note to a critic caused an uproar.
- Margaret married Clifton Daniel in 1956; four children.
- White House routine: early walks, swims, lunches with Bess; often vacationed with male friends.

THE AMERICAN FRANCHISE (https://millercenter.org/president/truman/the-american-franchise)
- Population about 150.7 million in 1950; nearly two-thirds urban or suburban.
- Average family size grew from 2.6 children (1940) to 3.2 (1950).
- Women: about 19 million worked outside the home in 1945; about 2 million left after the war.
- Suburbia: over 80% of nearly 2 million homes built 1945-1950 were in suburbs. Levittown (NY) had 17,000 houses.
- African Americans: about 1 million left the rural South in the 1940s. Supreme Court struck down segregation in interstate bus travel (1946) and restrictive housing covenants (1948). Jackie Robinson integrated MLB (1947).
- Labor: nearly 30% of workers in unions in 1945; decline after Taft-Hartley and the failed 1946 CIO "Operation Dixie."
- Red Scare: 13 states created their own HUAC equivalents; hundreds lost their jobs.

IMPACT AND LEGACY (https://millercenter.org/president/truman/impact-and-legacy)
- Low point when he left office (January 1953): unpopular amid the Korean War, corruption charges, and McCarthy's attacks.
- Reputation recovered: seen as a "feisty everyman from 'Middle America.'"
- Containment policy largely followed until the Cold War ended.
- Lasting commitments to Israel and South Korea; U.S. remains NATO's leading member.
- Managed the postwar shift; protected New Deal programs; raised the minimum wage (1949); expanded Social Security (1950).
- Civil rights: desegregated the military; banned discrimination in the civil service.
- Critics fault him on MacArthur, China policy, McCarthy, civil rights, and liberal programs. Hamby argues these critiques underestimate the divided Democratic Party and strong GOP opposition.

JOHN F. KENNEDY (35th President)
Essays by Marc J. Selverstone (Gerald L. Baliles Professor of Presidential Studies, Miller Center). Chicago citation: Miller Center of Public Affairs, University of Virginia. "John F. Kennedy." https://millercenter.org/president/kennedy
==================================================================

LIFE IN BRIEF (https://millercenter.org/president/kennedy/life-in-brief)
- Born into a wealthy, politically connected Irish-Catholic Boston family; eight siblings.
- Chronic illness in youth; wrote a best-selling book while a Harvard student.
- WWII: volunteered for hazardous Pacific duty; became a war hero.
- House of Representatives (1947-1953), then Senate (1953-1961).
- Youngest person elected president; first Roman Catholic president.
- Assassinated near the end of his third year in office (November 22, 1963).

LIFE BEFORE THE PRESIDENCY (https://millercenter.org/president/kennedy/life-before-the-presidency)
- Born 1917, the first 20th-century-born president. Father Joseph P. Kennedy made a fortune; FDR named him ambassador to the UK (1938).
- Harvard senior essay on British unpreparedness became the bestseller Why England Slept.
- August 1943: Japanese destroyer rammed PT-109; he led survivors on a three-mile swim; Navy and Marine Corps Medal; Purple Heart.
- Brother Joseph Jr. killed in Europe; family hopes shifted to John.
- 1946: won a Boston House seat at age 29; three terms.
- 1952: defeated Henry Cabot Lodge Jr. for the Senate. Married Jacqueline Bouvier in 1953.
- Managed Addison's disease, malaria, and back pain with painkillers and steroids.
- Profiles in Courage won the 1957 Pulitzer Prize for biography.
- 1956: narrowly lost the VP nomination to Estes Kefauver.

CAMPAIGNS AND ELECTIONS (https://millercenter.org/president/kennedy/campaigns-and-elections)
- 1960: Kennedy (43) vs. Nixon (47). Won the West Virginia primary over Hubert Humphrey.
- Chose Lyndon Johnson as running mate. Addressed doubts about his Catholic faith (Houston).
- Four televised debates (fall 1960); about 70 million viewed the first. Nixon looked pale and tense.
- Popular vote margin under 120,000 of 68.8 million cast. Electoral College 303 to 219.
- Inaugurated January 20, 1961: "the torch has been passed to a new generation of Americans."
- "New Frontiersmen": young, wealthy professionals and liberal intellectuals.

DOMESTIC AFFAIRS (https://millercenter.org/president/kennedy/domestic-affairs)
- "New Frontier" agenda (July 1960 convention speech); resisted by Southern Democratic committee chairs.
- Took office during the fourth postwar recession; 5.5 million unemployed.
- Congress raised the minimum wage; medical care for the elderly stalled; Appalachia development went forward.
- Education bill failed over aid to parochial schools.
- Civil rights: focused on enforcement; "senatorial courtesy" appointments of Southern judges.
- University of Mississippi (1962): Robert Kennedy federalized the Mississippi National Guard; James Meredith enrolled October 1, 1962, after riots.
- 1963: Birmingham violence, March on Washington (August), 16th Street church bombing (September); civil rights bill sent to Congress; became law after his death.

FOREIGN AFFAIRS (https://millercenter.org/president/kennedy/foreign-affairs)
- Bay of Pigs (April 17, 1961): about 1,500 Cuban exiles, CIA-backed plan inherited and altered; quickly defeated.
- Vienna summit with Khrushchev (June 1961); Berlin Wall built in August 1961.
- Cuban Missile Crisis (October 1962): naval blockade announced October 22. Khrushchev withdrew the missiles; U.S. secretly agreed to withdraw missiles from Turkey. Blockade lifted November 20, 1962.
- Vowed a moon landing by the decade's end; Manned Spacecraft Center located in Texas.
- Peace Corps (created 1961); Alliance for Progress for Latin America (March 1961).
- Vietnam: advisers rose to 16,000; Ngo Dinh Diem assassinated November 1963.
- Limited Test Ban Treaty (1963): his greatest accomplishment, in his view.

DEATH OF THE PRESIDENT (https://millercenter.org/president/kennedy/death-of-the-president)
- November 22, 1963, Dallas motorcade through Dealey Plaza; Governor John Connally also hit.
- Shots came from a nearby warehouse; Lee Harvey Oswald arrested; Jack Ruby shot Oswald two days later.
- Warren Commission (established by LBJ): Oswald acted alone.
- House Select Committee on Assassinations (1979): at least three shots; "probably assassinated as a result of a conspiracy."
- Hundreds of thousands viewed the casket in the Capitol rotunda. Buried at Arlington November 25; 92 nations sent representatives.

FAMILY LIFE (https://millercenter.org/president/kennedy/family-life)
- Caroline (born 1957); John Jr. (born November 1960). Patrick died two days after birth in August 1963.
- Robert Kennedy made attorney general ("some legal experience"). Congress later barred presidents from appointing close relatives.
- LBJ kept Robert off the 1964 ticket.
- Edward (Ted) won the Massachusetts Senate seat JFK vacated (1962).
- Robert won a New York Senate seat; shot by Sirhan Sirhan after the 1968 California primary.
- Edward challenged Jimmy Carter for the 1980 nomination and lost; senator until his death in 2009.

THE AMERICAN FRANCHISE (https://millercenter.org/president/kennedy/the-american-franchise)
- War on Poverty planning began in 1963 after Michael Harrington's The Other America; Johnson expanded it.
- Aimed at poor white Southerners in Appalachia and the rural South.
- As the first Catholic president, helped dispel "dual loyalty" accusations.
- Named Jews to his cabinet, federal courts, and the Supreme Court.
- Civil rights pace: promised to end housing discrimination "with the stroke of a pen," acted slowly; signed executive orders on federal contracts and property.
- Hispanic Americans about 2% of the population; little national attention.
- Backed the Equal Pay Act; created a commission on the status of women; few women in top federal positions.

IMPACT AND LEGACY (https://millercenter.org/president/kennedy/impact-and-legacy)
- "An unfinished life": promised much but never saw his program through; assessments remain mixed.
- Television began to shape voter behavior; style grew as important as substance.
- Early troubles: Bay of Pigs confirmed critics' doubts about inexperience.
- Fall 1962 turnaround: Cuba showdown, improving economy, partial test ban.
- Civil rights: federal intervention and activism slowly wore down segregationists.
- Vietnam deteriorated; no realistic plan.
- Historians: early New Frontiersmen were admiring; later studies focus on personal morals; recent works seek a middle ground.
