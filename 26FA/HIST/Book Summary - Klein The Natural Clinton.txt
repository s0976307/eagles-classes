STUDY SUMMARY: THE NATURAL: THE MISUNDERSTOOD PRESIDENCY OF BILL CLINTON
Joe Klein (Doubleday hardcover 2002; Broadway paperback 2003)

1. BOOK AND MAIN THESIS

- Author: Joe Klein, a political columnist (The New Yorker, Newsweek, later Time) who met Clinton in 1989 and wrote about him through both terms. He is an observer, not an official, and he admits his bias.
- Thesis: Clinton's presidency was misunderstood. Behind the scandals, the press obsession, and the partisan "ethics war" lay a serious policy record (deficit reduction, the EITC expansion, welfare reform, NAFTA, the 1997 budget deal, the Bosnia peace) built on a coherent "Third Way" philosophy. But Klein also argues the man was flawed: poll-driven, indecisive, self-indulgent, tactically brilliant but weak at articulating a vision, and self-destructive in the Lewinsky affair and the final pardons.
- Frame: policy vs. personality. Klein calls 1998 the "Clinton conundrum": solid policy and brilliant politics obscured by personal misconduct.

2. CHAPTER-BY-CHAPTER SUMMARY

Prologue (January 1998). The Lewinsky story breaks (Jan. 21), and the White House answers with "business as usual," staff focused on the State of the Union. In that speech (Jan. 27) Clinton proposes using the projected surplus to save Social Security first, forcing Republicans to applaud. Klein sets up the paradox: the most talented politician of his generation and a policy expert, who was also reckless and distrusted in Washington.

Chapter One (1989-1992: the New Democrat). Klein visits Hot Springs with Clinton's mother, Virginia Kelley, and hears about family alcoholism and therapy. The Democratic Leadership Council (DLC, 1985) and the Progressive Policy Institute (1989) aimed to pull Democrats away from interest-group "purity." Clinton became DLC chairman (1990-91). Reich's "sonar" image captures his pragmatism. The day-care fight (liberal ABC bill vs. a tax credit) produced the template: liberal ends through conservative means. The Cleveland DLC speech (May 1991) introduced the "new choice" theme. In 1992 he survived the Flowers and draft scandals; at the Richmond town meeting (Oct. 15) he stepped toward a questioner while Bush looked at his watch.

Chapter Two (transition and first year, 1992-1993). Early blunders: the gays-in-the-military pledge, a cabinet picked for demographic balance, and a weak first chief of staff (Mac McLarty). The economic team split between deficit hawks (Rubin, Altman, Shapiro) and investment liberals (Reich, Magaziner, Shearer). Clinton chose deficit reduction, aiming at the $500 billion Greenspan wanted, and rejected a stimulus. The budget passed the House by one vote and the Senate on Kerrey's vote; a gas tax replaced the Btu tax. The EITC expansion (about $15.9 billion to $21.2 billion) cut taxes for roughly 15 million working families, but the press largely missed it. Chaos stories and long, loose meetings dominated. Gergen joined the message operation in spring 1993.

Chapter Three (Gore and foreign policy, 1993-1995). Gore's Reinventing Government (REGO) cut about 350,000 federal jobs and 16,000 pages of rules, but Gore clashed with Hillary's health plan for priority. Early foreign policy was intermittent: Lake, Christopher, and Aspin were cautious. Somalia's "mission creep" ended in the Oct. 3, 1993 battle (18 Americans killed) and a U.S. withdrawal; the Haiti mission was turned back; Aspin resigned. Bosnia drifted until 1995, when cruise missiles hit Serb targets and Holbrooke brokered the Dayton accord, Klein's pick for the administration's most important foreign success. Klein's key point: the post-Somalia military would accept only low-risk, long-distance force.

Chapter Four (the ethics war and the press, 1984-1994). Klein traces the climate: the O'Neill-Gingrich clash over C-SPAN (May 1984); Speaker Jim Wright's resignation (1989); the Tower, Bork, and Thomas fights; post-Watergate laws (Independent Counsel, Ethics Act, Inspectors General) that empowered "Javert"-style special prosecutors; and a press that turned negative and personal. Clinton arrived in the middle of this war, and Whitewater, the Travel Office firings, and Troopergate were inflated. Vince Foster's suicide (July 13, 1993) deepened the Clintons' siege mentality. In December 1993 Stephanopoulos and Gergen urged releasing the Whitewater documents, but the Clintons refused; Clinton then asked for a special counsel (Fiske, later Starr). Klein judges that defiance self-defeating.

Chapter Five (marriage and health care, 1993-1995). Klein says the Clinton marriage can only be understood by believing all the stories at once: partners, rivals, lovers. Hillary was the policy force; her task force (with Magaziner) produced a 1,300-page employer-mandate plan, developed in secret and unwilling to compromise. Moynihan warned that universal coverage needed bipartisan consensus. Clinton later admitted a tax-credit approach would have been better; the GOP's Chafee plan (with Dole as cosponsor) was ignored. The January 1994 "veto pen" gesture hardened the impasse, and Greenberg's welfare-plus-crime alternative was rejected. Democrats lost both houses in November 1994. Clinton called it "my mistake, not hers"; Klein calls health care his greatest policy failure.

Chapter Six (survival and triangulation, 1994-1997). Leon Panetta became chief of staff in mid-1994 and imposed some order. Dick Morris (a secret adviser) and pollster Mark Penn drove policy through polling; Morris's "triangulation" became the label for Clinton's middle-ground strategy, which Klein calls shrewd given his lost credibility. Clinton moved fast on a $20 billion Mexico bailout (Jan. 1995) against public opinion. After Oklahoma City (April 19, 1995; 163 killed) and his May 5 Michigan State speech against anti-government extremism, he used "Smoke Them Out": let Republicans overreach, then attack. He held firm in the 1995-96 shutdowns, telling Armey he would never sign the Medicaid cuts. He signed welfare reform on July 31, 1996 over the objections of many aides and liberal allies; Gore said the system was "fundamentally broken" and the risk was "worth it." Klein credits the results: work among poor single mothers rose and marriage among black children increased. Clinton's incremental budget wins (Head Start, child care, EITC, AmeriCorps) culminated in the 1997 Balanced Budget Agreement: the first balanced budget in about 30 years, over $30 billion in college tax credits, and $24 billion for children's health insurance.

Chapter Seven (the Lewinsky scandal, 1998). Clinton's January denial; his later "legally accurate" but "incomplete" formulation; the Aug. 14 New York Times leak; and the Aug. 17 address, a grudging apology that attacked Starr. Klein says the communications machine broke down and Hillary's "it's your speech" signaled withdrawal. Cruise-missile strikes on Sudan and Afghanistan (Aug. 20) drew "Wag the Dog" suspicion but won public support. The Starr Report and the Sept. 21 grand jury tape ended the fever; Clinton's lawyerly calm ("depends on what the meaning of the word 'is' is") reversed the mood. Approval stayed around 60 percent, and about two-thirds opposed impeachment. Republicans lost five House seats; Gingrich and then Livingston resigned. Klein's theories: Skowronek's "Third Way presidents" (Wilson, Nixon, Clinton) get demonized by opponents; Rene Girard's scapegoat theory says Clinton absorbed his generation's vices, but the sacrifice produced no catharsis.

Chapter Eight (second term: economy, globalism, terrorism, Middle East, pardons, 1997-2001). Ira Magaziner's Internet framework (July 1, 1997) kept e-commerce tax-free and unregulated; Gore and the FCC pushed competition, and the 1996 Telecommunications Act helped. Clinton was "cyber-impaired" but understood economic change. On terrorism, Klein lists Khobar Towers (June 1996, 19 killed), the embassy bombings (Aug. 7, 1998, 263 killed), and the USS Cole (Oct. 12, 2000, 17 killed). The August 1998 strike on bin Laden "just missed"; a plan to freeze terrorist bank accounts was blocked by Treasury; the FBI and White House clashed. Lewinsky's policy shadow: the Dec. 16, 1998 Iraq bombing during impeachment, the 1999 Kosovo air war, a flip-flop on China trade, no Social Security or Medicare reform, and the failed Camp David summit (summer 2000), where Klein says Clinton misread Arafat. On his last night, Clinton granted 177 pardons, including Marc Rich, whose ex-wife Denise Rich and friend Beth Dozoretz had courted the White House. Klein calls the pardons a favor for flattery, tied to the same need for attention that drove Lewinsky.

Conclusion (unlabeled in the scan, pp. 208-217). Clinton "remains the most compelling politician of his generation," though that is a low bar next to Quayle, Gore, Gingrich, Hastert, Lott, Daschle, George W. Bush, and Blair. Klein blames a generation "untested" by hardship and a prosperous age that did not demand greatness. Polling and marketing produced followers, not leaders (Penn: Lincoln Bedroom stories reached 8 percent of voters; the Mars landing 93 percent). Clinton's best impulse was civic (national service, "My fellow Americans"), but AmeriCorps was watered down and Kyoto went unimplemented. Klein's bottom line: he saw the world clearly and explained a huge economic change, but his legacy may be "smug, shallow serenity."

5. ASSESSMENT: STRENGTHS AND FAILURES (KLEIN'S VIEW)

Strengths: command of policy and a gift for explaining complex issues; economic courage (deficit reduction despite political risk, a bet that paid off); a Third Way synthesis (work-based welfare reform, the EITC); persistence in incremental budget wins over six years; diplomacy (Bosnia and Dayton, the Oklahoma City response); and a real empathy that connected with ordinary people (Richmond, Memphis).

Failures: a chaotic first year (slow decisions, long meetings, weak staff, the gays-in-the-military start); a secret, uncompromising health process; early retreats to Congress (the Btu tax, congressional reforms); defiance toward the press that let Whitewater feed on itself; poll-driven positioning with no clear public vision; caution and remote-control force in foreign policy (Somalia, slow Bosnia response); personal conduct (Lewinsky, false statements, pardons); and missed chances (no Social Security or Medicare reform; a watered-down AmeriCorps; "gestures" rather than sustained leadership).

6. LIKELY TEST QUESTIONS (SHORT ANSWERS)

Q: What is Klein's central argument?
A: Clinton's policy record was serious and underrated, but scandal and a partisan media war overshadowed it. His personal failings and lack of a clear, inspiring vision were also real.

Q: What was the Third Way, and what formula did it use?
A: Clinton's synthesis of New Democrat ideas (opportunity, responsibility, community). Its formula was "liberal ends through conservative means," seen in the day-care tax credit, the EITC, and welfare reform.

Q: Why did Clinton choose deficit reduction in 1993?
A: He believed lower deficits would lower interest rates and unlock private investment. Greenspan's credibility required about $500 billion in cuts. It was a risky bet on unproven theory, and Klein calls it courageous.

Q: What was the EITC expansion?
A: A 1993 increase (about $15.9 billion to $21.2 billion) that cut taxes for roughly 15 million working families. It went largely unnoticed, which is why Klein calls it a hidden "middle-class tax cut."

Q: Why did health care fail?
A: A secret, Hillary-led task force produced a 1,300-page employer-mandate plan that was complicated, uncompromising, and sold badly. Moynihan's warning was ignored, the Chafee plan was dismissed, and Clinton later admitted it was "my mistake."

Q: What was the "ethics war"?
A: An escalating partisan use of investigations, special prosecutors, ethics charges, and press attacks, driven by post-Watergate reforms and Gingrich's guerrilla tactics. Klein's "Javert" image describes prosecutors who expanded their mandates without limit.

Q: What was "Smoke Them Out," and why did it matter?
A: Clinton's strategy of letting Republicans overreach (the shutdowns, Medicare and Medicaid cuts) and then exposing them. Klein credits it with turning Clinton from weak to dominant after 1995.

Q: Why did Clinton sign welfare reform?
A: He had promised to "end welfare as we know it," and it was central to his New Democrat credibility. Gore backed signing. Klein says outcomes were positive, though some provisions were harsh.

Q: What was the significance of Somalia?
A: The October 1993 battle (18 Americans killed) showed "mission creep" and a military unwilling to take risks. Clinton withdrew, and the episode made the military and the White House wary of close combat.

Q: What is the scapegoat theory?
A: Rene Girard's idea, which Klein applies to Clinton: a society sacrifices a figure who embodies its vices to relieve guilt. Clinton absorbed the excesses of his generation, but the Lewinsky sacrifice brought no catharsis.

Q: How did the Lewinsky affair shape policy?
A: Klein links it to the August 1998 cruise-missile strikes, the December 1998 Iraq bombing, the Kosovo war, the China trade reversal, and the lost chance at Social Security and Medicare reform.

Q: What is Klein's final judgment?
A: A serious policy record and real economic achievements, but a president who sought approval over leadership. Scandal and his own choices shrank his legacy to "smug, shallow serenity."
